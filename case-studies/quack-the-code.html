Case Study

QuackTheCode

An educational game that treats each programming exercise as a level objective, built so beginners get feedback on their reasoning rather than a bare pass or fail.

Role UX Design & Development
Tools UX Flow, Figma, JavaScript
Timeline In development
Scope Level design, UX, front-end build

The problem

Most beginner coding practice is a wall of text and a blank editor. Learners hit an error, get told the answer is wrong, and have no way to tell whether they misread the problem, misunderstood the concept, or simply made a typo.

That ambiguity is where people quit. A correct answer teaches less than an incorrect one that explains what went wrong, so I designed QuackTheCode around making the failure state the most informative screen in the product.

Approach

I mapped the full player journey before drawing any screens, looking specifically for every point where a learner could be stuck and have no way forward. The level structure came out of that map.

  1. Journey map

    Traced a first-time player from opening the game to completing their first challenge, marking every point of uncertainty and every dead end.

  2. Level curve

    Sequenced challenges from syntax recall up to multi-step logic, introducing one new idea at a time rather than testing several at once.

  3. Error states

    Wrote the failure states first, then designed around them — deciding what each wrong answer should teach before deciding what success looks like.

Key design decisions

The brief never leaves the screen. The challenge description sits beside the editor rather than on a previous screen. Beginners lose the thread of a multi-step objective the moment they have to remember it, and the layout removes that opportunity entirely.

Errors explain, they don't reject. When code fails, the game names what it was looking for and points at the relevant line. A wrong answer is the moment with the most teaching value, so it gets the most deliberate design.

One idea per level. Each challenge introduces a single concept. Bundling concepts made early levels feel harder than they were and produced failures players couldn't attribute to anything they'd just learned.

Outcome

The game is still in progress, with the level structure, core loop, and error-state pattern established. The remaining work is content — more challenges and levels — rather than redesign.

Replace this with any play-test findings you have. In particular, the error states are the part most worth testing with someone who has never written code.

What I'd do next

The biggest gap is that the game has no way to show progress. A learner who closes the tab has no record of what they solved, and a level list without any sense of movement removes most of the motivation the game design is built on. That is the first thing I'd add.
